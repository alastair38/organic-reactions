Higher coreOxidation

Oxidation of alcohols

The position of the –OH group decides the product. Primary alcohols climb the oxidation ladder; secondary alcohols stop at a ketone; tertiary alcohols do not react with these oxidising agents.

CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O
CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH
CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O
Reagents
Hot copper(II) oxide, CuO, or acidified dichromate(VI), usually K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄.
Conditions
Heat. Use excess oxidant and reflux for the carboxylic acid; distil the aldehyde as it forms for partial oxidation.
Observation
Dichromate: orange → green. CuO: black → brown solid.
Key pattern
Primary → aldehyde → acid; secondary → ketone; tertiary → no reaction.
Exam tip: the “no reaction” trap

Always check the carbon carrying the –OH group. A tertiary alcohol has no hydrogen on that carbon, so it cannot be oxidised by these reagents. Do not say “tertiary alcohols are unreactive” in general — they simply do not react with these oxidising agents.

Higher coreOxidationTests

Testing for aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes can be oxidised further; ketones cannot (under these conditions). That difference gives three useful tests.

Aldehyde + [O] → carboxylic acid
Ketone + [O] → no further oxidation
Fehling's
Blue solution → brick-red precipitate with an aldehyde; no change with a ketone.
Tollens'
Clear, colourless reagent → silver mirror with an aldehyde; no change with a ketone.
Dichromate
Orange → green with an aldehyde; stays orange with a ketone.
Why
The aldehyde has a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon that can be removed as oxidation proceeds; the ketone does not.
Exam tip: describe, then explain

“Brick-red precipitate” and “silver mirror” are observations. If the question asks you to explain, link the observation to the aldehyde being oxidised to a carboxylic acid. Do not just name the test.

Higher coreEstersCondensation

Esterification

An alcohol and a carboxylic acid join together, lose water and form an ester. The reaction is reversible, so conditions and product removal matter.

alcohol + carboxylic acid ⇌ ester + water
CH₃CH₂OH + CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O
Type
Condensation (esterification). Not addition — a small molecule is eliminated.
Catalyst
Concentrated sulfuric acid. It speeds up the reaction and helps remove water.
Naming
Alcohol → –yl; acid → –oate. Ethanol + ethanoic acid → ethyl ethanoate.
Uses
Flavourings, fragrances and solvents for non-polar compounds.
Exam tip: reversible arrow and yield

The reaction is reversible. Removing the ester as it forms (or removing water) shifts the equilibrium to the right and increases the yield. A catalyst does not change the equilibrium position — it only helps equilibrium arrive sooner.

Higher coreHydrolysis

Hydrolysis of esters

Water breaks the ester link. Acid hydrolysis gives the carboxylic acid and alcohol; alkaline hydrolysis gives a carboxylate salt and alcohol.

ester + water ⇌ alcohol + carboxylic acid
ester + NaOH → alcohol + sodium carboxylate
Acid hydrolysis
Reversible. Produces the parent alcohol and carboxylic acid.
Alkaline hydrolysis
Effectively irreversible. Produces the alcohol and the carboxylate salt; acidify to get the acid.
Fats and oils
Alkaline hydrolysis of a triglyceride is saponification: glycerol + three soap molecules.
Exam language
Hydrolysis breaks a bond using water. Condensation is the reverse: joins molecules while eliminating water.
Exam tip: read the condition before naming products

If the question says “hydrolysed with water”, name the acid and alcohol. If it says “hydrolysed with sodium hydroxide”, name the sodium carboxylate salt and the alcohol. This is a common source of lost marks.

Higher coreFats & oilsAddition

Fats, oils and the bromine test

Fats and oils are triglycerides. Their fatty acid chains may be saturated or unsaturated. Bromine adds across C=C bonds, so the test reveals unsaturation.

unsaturated oil + Br₂ → brominated oil
orange-brown → colourless
Structure
Triglyceride: glycerol backbone + three fatty acid chains joined by ester links.
Melting point
More unsaturation → more kinks → poorer packing → weaker London dispersion forces → lower melting point.
Test
Bromine water is decolourised quickly by an unsaturated oil; a saturated fat does not decolourise it.
Type
Addition across the C=C double bond.
Exam tip: “decolourised”, not “clear”

Say the orange-brown bromine solution is decolourised. If you write “it goes clear”, you may lose the mark because “clear” can mean transparent, not colourless. Also link the result to addition across C=C.

Higher coreSoapsEmulsions

Soaps, micelles and emulsions

Alkaline hydrolysis of a fat or oil produces soap. The soap ion has a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail, which is why it can lift grease into water.

triglyceride + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3 soap
soap ion = R–COO⁻Na⁺
Formation
Alkaline hydrolysis (saponification) of a fat or oil. The fatty acids are neutralised to ionic salts.
Micelle
Hydrophobic tails dissolve in grease; hydrophilic heads stay in water. Agitation suspends the grease.
Hard water
Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions form an insoluble precipitate (scum) with soap ions.
Emulsifier
Has hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts; stabilises an emulsion instead of letting layers separate.
Exam tip: draw the micelle, label both ends

If you are asked to explain cleaning action, use the words hydrophobic tail, hydrophilic head, micelle and suspension. A diagram with labels is often quicker and clearer than a long paragraph.

Higher coreProteinsCondensation

Proteins: condensation and hydrolysis

Amino acids join by condensation to form peptide links. Hydrolysis reverses the process. Denaturation is different: it changes shape without breaking peptide bonds.

amino acid + amino acid → dipeptide + H₂O
protein + water → amino acids (hydrolysis)
Functional groups
Amino acids contain –NH₂ and –COOH. The R group differs between amino acids.
Condensation
The –NH₂ of one amino acid reacts with the –COOH of another, forming –CONH– and water.
Hydrolysis
Water breaks peptide links, producing amino acids. Enzymes do this during digestion.
Denaturation
Heat or pH changes break intermolecular forces and change shape; peptide bonds stay intact.
Exam tip: count the water molecules

Joining n amino acids in one chain forms n − 1 peptide links and releases n − 1 water molecules. If a question gives four amino acids, look for three peptide links.

Higher coreFree radicalsSkin care

Free radical chain reactions

UV light can break bonds and create radicals. Radical chain reactions have three stages: initiation, propagation and termination.

initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl•
propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl
termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
Free radical
An atom or molecule with an unpaired electron; highly reactive.
Initiation
Radicals are created, often when UV light breaks a bond homolytically.
Propagation
A radical is used and another is produced, so the chain continues.
Termination
Two radicals combine; no radical remains to continue the chain.
Exam tip: classify by what happens to the radical

If radicals appear where there were none, it is initiation. If a radical is on both sides, it is propagation. If radicals disappear into a stable molecule, it is termination. Scavengers are molecules that react with radicals to form stable products.

Higher coreAcids + bases

Carboxylic acids reacting with bases

Carboxylic acids are weak acids but they still neutralise metal oxides, hydroxides and carbonates. Long-chain fatty acids form soaps when neutralised by alkali.

acid + metal oxide → salt + water
acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water
acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + CO₂
Salt naming
Ethanoic acid → ethanoate; propanoic acid → propanoate.
Carbonate test
Carbon dioxide is produced; it turns limewater milky.
Soap link
Long-chain fatty acid + NaOH → sodium salt of the fatty acid (soap) + water.
Weak acid
Carboxylic acids are weak acids: they are only partially ionised in water, so their pH is higher than a strong acid of the same concentration.
Exam tip: balance the carbonate equation

Carbonate reactions produce two carboxylic acid salt units per carbonate ion, so remember the coefficient 2 in front of the acid and the salt. Check that atoms and charges balance.

ExtensionBridge to Advanced Higher

Alkenes, dehydration and reduction

These transformations are useful for understanding the bigger organic map. They are not all mandatory in the current Higher course, so treat this as a bridge to National 5 prior learning and Advanced Higher.

alkene + H₂ → alkane (Ni catalyst)
alkene + Br₂ → dibromoalkane
alkene + H₂O → alcohol (acid catalyst)
alcohol → alkene + H₂O (Al₂O₃ or conc. H₂SO₄)
Why include it?
It completes the functional-group map and explains why alkenes are described as unsaturated.
Current Higher
Bromine addition to C=C is core. The other transformations are extension / prior learning unless your teacher says otherwise.
Advanced Higher
You will meet mechanisms, Markovnikov's rule and reduction of carbonyls in more detail.
Exam tip: label extension answers clearly

In a Higher exam, answer the question that is asked. Use the extension card to deepen understanding of the map, but do not replace the core Higher reactions with Advanced Higher detail unless it is relevant.
